# Skip-test residue — Section 1 (Introduction), Session 2

Where do strategic theories come from? Consider a firm that has just lost a market it was certain it understood. Its theory of demand did not merely mispredict; every account the firm knows how to state has now failed, and the distinction that would explain the defeat is one it cannot yet draw. The firm's problem is not to update a belief. It is to acquire a language in which the right belief could be expressed. This paper is about that problem.

The theory-based view of strategy holds that economic actors are theorists: managers and entrepreneurs carry causal accounts of value creation, and those accounts—not merely information or incentives—direct what they notice, test, and do (Felin and Zenger 2017; Felin, Gambardella and Zenger 2024). The view now has a substantial formal and empirical apparatus. Theories guide experimentation and search (Camuffo et al. 2020; Chavda, Gans and Stern 2024), support the acquisition of resources and rents (Ehrig and Zenger 2024), and discipline entrepreneurial decisions in the field (Camuffo et al. 2024; Ott and Hannah 2024). But the formal treatments begin downstream: with a menu of theories to test, a causal conjecture to search under, or an awareness advantage to exploit. Felin, Gambardella and Zenger (2024) explicitly flag the upstream step—where a genuinely new theory comes from—as the open analytical problem of the program. This paper models that step.

The step is hard for a precise reason: it sits at a boundary of Bayesian rationality. Bayesian updating ranks the theories an actor can already express. It cannot produce a distinction never yet drawn, because conditioning reweights a space of possibilities and no reweighting enlarges the space. Nor does updating sound an alarm when the space itself is broken: posterior mass always sums to one, so a manager whose every expressible theory is false can grow steadily more confident in the least false among them. [Footnote: Under misspecification the posterior concentrates on the expressible theories closest to the data-generating process (Berk 1966); Esponda and Pouzo (2016) develop the equilibrium counterpart. Ortoleva (2012) models paradigm shifts in which a surprised agent switches priors, but his agent switches among alternatives represented ex ante; here the replacement is inexpressible before insight.] Discovering that one's causal language has failed, and repairing it, are therefore different cognitive operations from ranking the answers the language allows—and only the ranking is Bayesian.

The model supplies the missing transition as a complete loop, displayed in the chain figure. Agents hold theories on a coarse causal language, judge them by Bayes' rule, act through standing policies, and experience the consequences; this back end is standard subjective rationality (Kalai and Lehrer 1993; Ryall 2003). The front end makes the language itself answerable to experience. Causal dissonance is a separate, statistically fallible diagnosis that every theory the agent can state has failed its own evidence. Dissonance poses a question the current language cannot answer; the agent may then elect inquiry, a costly and fallible process that operates on remembered particulars and may deliver a distinction the agent could not previously draw. Formulation assigns causal content to the refined language, a bridge prior—a fresh prior on the expanded language—restores a probability space, and Bayesian judgment resumes. The creative act itself, how a mind produces a new predicate, is treated as a primitive technology; the model explains when insight is demanded, what constrains it, what it is worth, and what its output warrants. Throughout, a two-firm, four-state market example serves as laboratory: each firm chooses one of two product architectures, customer configurations determine which architecture wins, and neither firm initially perceives the distinction that governs demand.

The first finding is that experience directs repair without determining it. When the example's firms differentiate and the market delivers a win at one customer configuration and a loss at another, the record tells each firm exactly what any new theory must explain: those two experiences can no longer be treated as alike. That requirement has teeth—of the fifteen ways to partition the example's four states, eleven are ruled out. But four repairs remain, and the two simplest, organizing the market by row or by column, fit the record equally well and cannot both be right. Worlds that agree on everything the firms have ever seen can require different repairs, so no procedure that reads only the record—the model calls such procedures truth-blind—can be reliably correct; in the example, the best any truth-blind technology can guarantee is a coin flip. Insight is necessarily fallible, and the fallibility is not a defect of the inquirer but the price of a representation that economizes on distinctions.

The second finding is that a firm can rationally pay for a distinction it cannot yet name. At dissonance the firm cannot compute an expected value over candidate answers, because the candidates are not representable objects. It can, however, read its own record: situations like the ones it lost will recur, and a distinction that sorted them would let it act on them. Deliberative leverage—the value of a question—prices exactly this: the robust gain from letting action vary across remembered experiences, over and above the best the firm could do by adjusting one action within its current categories. In the example that value is exactly one half, for both firms, at the moment their shared language collapses. Leverage prices action separation, not uncertainty reduction, and the difference shows in who inquires: after a later stretch of play, the firm holding the true theory faces no unresolved action margin and values its question at zero, while its defeated rival values the same question at one half. Success, not ignorance, is what extinguishes the demand for new theories.

The third finding is that generating a theory and validating it are different achievements, and the evidence for the first cannot double as evidence for the second. A successful repair fits the record that provoked it—by construction, since fitting the record is the entry requirement for a repair. Retrospective fit therefore carries no evidential weight of its own: conditional on the record and the inquiry history, the output of a truth-blind technology is independent of which world is actual. In the example, a firm that adopts the unique theory consistent with its refined language holds it with subjective confidence one, while a reflective assessor who has seen the same record assigns it probability one half: the gap between confidence and warranted confidence is exactly plus one half, and it is the same whether the adopted theory happens to be true or false. The firm cannot close this gap by itself, because computing it requires representing rival worlds its language cannot express. Validation must come from new evidence that discriminates—which gives boards, investors, and other broader-awareness outsiders a precise epistemic role that retrospective coherence cannot play.

The fourth finding is that strategic heterogeneity can originate before priors. Inquiry is an economic process, and it can die in every way an economic process dies: the question is answered, it is priced below its cost, it is abandoned after repeated failure, or it was never answerable. The example displays the resulting endings. Under perfectly forgiving demand the two firms can settle permanently on the same false theory, each earning one half forever, because their pooled play conceals the error; the market never generates the contrast that would expose it. Under even slightly punishing demand that trap becomes a knife edge, and an asymmetric ending appears instead: one firm holds the truth and earns three quarters while its rival earns one quarter, permanently. The disadvantaged firm sits in dissonant stasis—aware that its account has failed, yet rationally unwilling to pay for a question priced below its cost. A third ending requires no ignorance at all: a firm can acquire the true theory and still hold its old policy, because the switch it now knows to be an improvement does not clear its switching cost—enlightened inertia. Identical firms facing identical environments can thus end with different languages, different theories, and permanently different payoffs, for reasons that precede any prior.

The contribution is therefore upstream of the existing formal apparatus of the theory-based view. Models of theory testing, theory-guided search, and awareness advantage each begin with a representational achievement in hand; this paper supplies the passage that produces it—from the failure of a causal language, through a priced question and a fallible repair, back to Bayesian judgment—and shows that the passage itself generates competitively consequential heterogeneity. Two boundaries should be stated plainly. The model treats the semantic act of insight as a primitive: it explains the conditions, direction, price, and warrant of representational change, not the conscious creative operation itself. And the vocabulary of elementary causal mechanisms is fixed throughout, so insight here means discovering which differences matter, with expansion of the vocabulary itself reserved for companion work in this research program.
